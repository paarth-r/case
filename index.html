F. Scott Fitzgerald · Esquire · February 1937

An Alcoholic Case

Aidan · Paarth · Sol
On the distance between understanding someone and being able to save them.
Central Theme

Fitzgerald explores the limits of one person's ability to save another.

The nurse's compassion for the man is genuine — but she overestimates what her compassion can actually do for him. Over the course of the case her understanding of him deepens, and every inch of that understanding costs her a piece of her confidence.

The claim

Understanding someone's struggle does not give you the power to change their actions.

01She Believes She Can Fix Him

The nurse arrives with the confidence of a technician.

  • She sees him first as a case — another alcoholic, a category she has handled before.
  • Her plan is procedural: stop the drinking, keep him fed, keep him out of harm.
  • She takes the assignment believing the problem is the bottle.
I guess if I hadn't liked him I wouldn't have stayed on the case. Narration — the nurse's own thought
Analysis

Her original approach can only reach the surface. Nothing in it addresses why he drinks — so from the first page, her method is aimed at the wrong target.

02The Window

The broken bottle is the closest they ever come to seeing each other.

  • They wrestle over the gin. It slips — a flash of red and black, SIR GALAHAD, DISTILLED LOUISVILLE GIN — and shatters on the bathroom floor.
  • His first reaction is not for the drink. It is for her: "Then I'll step on the broken glass — or you'll step on it."
  • For one moment she stops seeing an alcoholic and sees a man.
The glass, in its fragments, was less than a window through which they had seen each other for a moment. An Alcoholic Case

A window shows you what was hidden — but less than a window. She gets a glimpse, not an understanding. It is already broken when she looks through it.

Analysis

Her new sympathy makes her more invested in helping him. It does not make her more able to.

03The Copper Plate

Helping him change clothes, she finds the plate over his heart.

  • A copper plate on his left rib, "about the size of a silver dollar."
  • He puts his cigarette out on it — deliberately, "like a dagger against his heart."
  • The drinking is one symptom of a self-destruction that has already found other doors.
It crushed out against a copper plate on his left rib about the size of a silver dollar. An Alcoholic Case
Analysis

Take away every bottle in the room and the plate is still there. Her fuller understanding of his suffering is exactly what shows her the ceiling on her own usefulness.

04She Reaches Her Limit

He looks at the corner where the bottle broke, and she understands.

  • Fitzgerald writes the thing he is facing as something he can nearly see in the room.
  • She recognizes it is larger than alcohol — and that effort is not the currency it takes.
  • She only puts it into words afterward, back at the agency, trying to explain to Mrs. Hixson why she cannot take the case again.
It's not like anything you can beat — no matter how hard you try. The nurse, to Mrs. Hixson
Analysis

Compassion is not control. She can care for him, understand him, even move him — but she cannot make his choices for him.

Conclusion
It's all for nothing. The nurse — final line of the story

She is not saying he was worthless. She is saying her effort could not reach him. Fitzgerald — writing in 1936, four years from his own death — ends on the gap between caring about someone and being able to save them.

The Author

F. Scott Fitzgerald, 1896–1940.

  • Born September 24, 1896 in St. Paul, Minnesota; schooled at the Newman School in New Jersey, then Princeton, which he left to join the U.S. Army in World War I.
  • Stationed in Alabama, he met Zelda Sayre; they married in 1920, the year This Side of Paradise made him famous.
  • New York, then Paris — where he fell in with the Lost Generation, the writers left adrift by the war.
  • The Jazz Age he named ran on nightlife and drinking. His own addiction ran on stress, insecurity, and pressure — and it cost him his health, his finances, his writing, and his marriage.
  • Zelda's mental illness took her into treatment for most of the 1930s.
  • Best known for The Great Gatsby; ended as a Hollywood screenwriter. Died of a heart attack on December 21, 1940, at 44.
Why it matters here

"An Alcoholic Case" ran in Esquire in February 1937. Fitzgerald wrote the patient from the inside.

Journal Prompt

You have one week.

You have one week to help a person who is making a decision that will seriously hurt their future — and you know you cannot make them listen to you. Choose the person, what they are doing, and why you care about them.

Write a short scene from your perspective

What do you say? What makes you believe you can help them? And at what point do you realize the decision is ultimately theirs?
